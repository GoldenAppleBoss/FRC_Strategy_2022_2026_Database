# 1323 in 2025: Aurelia’s value depends on the alliance around it

The base dossier describes a compact tilting elevator, ground acquisition, a differential end effector and dedicated L1 work. That list does not explain why the package was useful. The deeper story concerns where mass and motion were placed, how transitions were controlled, how the robot was practiced, and how a highly capable scoring robot could rationally spend important matches doing a low-valued task. This supplement combines team replies from the release discussion with attributed match interpretation. It does not certify every configuration or CAD revision.

## Ground acquisition changes the source of the cycle, not only the pickup

The team credits Spectrum’s published funneling prototypes as direct input to its thinking. That is a specific lineage claim by the team, stronger than an observer noticing a resemblance. [Acknowledgment](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/212).

A ground collector becomes strategically powerful only when the rest of the system can exploit it. The human player has to deliver useful coral near the intended work area; the robot has to acquire it in more than one plausible orientation; the handoff must preserve control; and the scoring motion must finish before the next piece is needed. Reducing travel can expose a new bottleneck in transfer or joint motion. The agent should therefore compare complete acquisition-to-next-acquisition cycles rather than assume a floor pickup automatically halves cycle time.

The RoboReplay review ledger preserves a progression from early floor-supplied cycles to widespread field supply, then to L1 placement and algae security. These are dated observations and interpretations, not proof that 1323 invented each tactic first. The recap author explicitly does not claim to know the first inventor. Use that ledger to find the relevant discussion, then use team releases for mechanism construction and official records for scores.

## A differential needs a coordinate system and a recovery plan

In its controls explanation, the team describes two CANcoders and average/difference relationships for pitch and roll, with useful resolution through the range of motion. It also describes pain caused by a poor zeroing arrangement on the practice machine. [Encoder explanation](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/218).

The transferable issue is not the encoder brand. Coupled actuation makes a light moving assembly possible, but makes calibration, limits and fault recovery part of the mechanism. If a team loses one joint reference, it needs to know which physical configurations are still safe. A robot that must disassemble a gearbox or trust an eyeballed pose after every power cycle can spend far more competition time recovering than its nominal mechanism speed saves.

The thread’s belt discussion also keeps packaging costs visible. The team reports more tension than expected and explains the role of belt construction and wrap. A later answer discusses the packaging that reduced wrap and the use of current/velocity when detecting a limit, while suggesting a larger pulley or changed path as a potential improvement. [Belt experience](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/242). [Packaging discussion](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/250). Do not copy a thin, low-mass elevator rendering without the tension, stiffness, bearing loads and reference strategy that support it.

## Mechanically possible motion is not the same as permitted match motion

The team explains an emphasis on continuous joint motion toward useful states rather than a sequence of pauses. It also describes driver overrides for L1 and handoff behavior, a later allowance for algae-intake positioning, and Worlds changes including additional vision for L1 and default stow behavior. These are control-policy changes, not evidence that every physically reachable pose was always exposed to the driver. [Operating policy](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/236).

Another reply describes a superstructure state containing elevator position, elevator pivot and wrist pitch/roll, with a choreographer that builds safe transitions. A separate answer discusses using differential controller support once the mechanics and sensing were correct. [State representation](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/283). [Controller/mechanical dependency](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/281).

An agent recommending this architecture should explicitly include that transition layer. A tilting elevator with a versatile wrist can have excellent final poses and still collide with its own intake during the path between them. The architecture should name which motions can overlap, which require a clearance condition, what the operator can interrupt, and where a stalled transition leaves the game piece. This is a reason to study code releases alongside CAD, while preserving their exact commit and event relevance. A public repository’s current default branch is not automatically the version run at a particular event.

## Human control remained part of the successful system

The team describes left/right branch selection relative to the nearest reef location and explains that manual release could be cleaner than a fully automatic release because of mechanism quirks. Driver and operator coordination also helped prevent intake interference with pipes. [Driver interface](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/303).

This avoids a common generalization error: a high-performing robot is not necessarily autonomous in every part of teleop. A deliberately manual action can be a reasonable way to handle ambiguous sensing or preserve operator judgment. The relevant measure is the entire decision-and-execution loop, including its error rate under contact and visual obstruction. The agent should not recommend automatic release merely because the mechanism has accurate joints. It should ask whether the observations needed to release safely are actually available.

## The build history includes abandoned mechanisms

The team’s timeline describes early CAD blocks, a largely functional machine by the following phase, and subsequent redesign. It names claw and hanger work, removal of suction, later mass reduction, a 2910-inspired hanger and Worlds emphasis on robustness and weight. [Development account](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/263).

The abandoned suction matters. A database that gathers every impressive prototype and treats all of them as features of the final robot produces an impossible composite. The correct representation is a versioned choice: a concept existed, a later report says it was removed, and the final package must be determined from the dated sources. Similarly, “2910 hanger” is a lineage lead that still needs the year and exact retained principles. It is not permission to assume identical dimensions or identical integration into a different superstructure.

## Practice and material selection are part of the architecture’s cost

The team describes building the practice robot early enough for extensive validation, while the competition machine was completed much closer to its event. It also notes configuration drift between two robots as a cost. [Two-robot account](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/267). A later reply gives a rough team estimate about the benefit of their practice time; this is a self-assessment, not a controlled measurement or a universal hours-to-performance equation. [Practice reflection](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/305).

Another reply contrasts inexpensive, quickly iterated practice prints with competition material chosen for confidence, while noting that practice hardware accumulated more hours. [Printing experience](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/327). Material choice therefore has to be read with the test exposure, replacement cadence and failure consequence. A competition material does not by itself explain the success of the mechanism; rapid cheap prototypes may have discovered the load path and geometry that made the final part straightforward.

## Why specializing in L1 can be the correct job for an excellent robot

Once partners can reliably fill the higher branches, an additional robot’s ability to place another L4 has little immediate value unless it frees a partner for a better job. Fast L1 acquisition and controlled placement can instead increase the alliance’s remaining score. Orientation and release geometry affect how densely coral can be arranged near a convenient face, which changes how soon the robot must move to a less convenient face. This is an alliance-dependent marginal-value argument, not a claim that L1 is intrinsically the best task.

The RoboReplay finals analysis attributes importance to 1323’s L1 stacking and 2910’s algae steals. It also interprets score visibility and drive-team decisions; those psychological explanations remain commentary. See the complete review of `SQure1sSDpk` in the RoboReplay ledger for the source, with official Einstein records used for scores. A future agent should preserve the difference between the visible configuration, a measured cycle count, a team statement about its plan and an analyst’s explanation of the result.

## What to borrow and what to test

Retrieve this case for low moving mass, coupled joints, bidirectional scoring, dedicated L1 placement and the distinction between capability and match role. Test the collection/handoff cycle with delivered coral at realistic locations, joints moving simultaneously, contact during acquisition, worn components and an interrupted transition. Validate zeroing and recovery as deliberately as scoring. Pair the case with 2910’s arm architecture and 1690’s low-mass vacuum/differential system to compare where each design puts its complexity. The agent should state which capability your likely PCH partners supply; without that assumption, a recommendation to optimize L1 may copy the visible job while missing the alliance that made it valuable.
