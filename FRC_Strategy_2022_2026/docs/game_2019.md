# 2019 — DESTINATION: DEEP SPACE

## A finite set of coupled scoring states

DEEP SPACE combines hatch installation, cargo delivery, blind initial operation and a climb onto stepped platforms. Its repeated cycle is not equivalent to pouring more objects into a common goal. Every useful destination has a state: an empty hatch location, a retained panel with an empty bay, a completed panel/cargo pair, or a location already occupied. A robot that handles only one piece type can exhaust its useful targets or become dependent on another robot's prerequisites.

The governing rules source here is the [official 133-page manual](https://firstfrc.blob.core.windows.net/frc2019/Manual/2019FRCGameSeasonManual.pdf), notably physical PDF pages 22–34, 39–43 and 52–60. This is an archived assembled manual; verify the governing update/Q&A for a particular competition. All illustrative throughput and marginal calculations below are analysis, not claimed observations of specific matches.

The first 15 seconds are SANDSTORM: driver view is blocked, but a team may use autonomous behavior, cameras or blind driving. Do not describe it as an autonomous-only phase or apply a different piece scoring multiplier. Leaving from HAB level 1 earns 3 and from level 2 earns 6 when the crossing conditions are met. The remaining 135 seconds are normal driver control. A hatch panel is worth 2 and a retained cargo is worth 3. End-of-match HAB levels 1/2/3 are worth 3/6/12. Complete Rocket requires six scored panels and six cargo in one rocket; HAB Docking requires at least 15 climb points. Each grants one qualification RP, in addition to match result RP. These bonuses do not carry into playoffs.

Hatch retention is a real prerequisite. A cargo counts only in a bay with a scored hatch or null hatch, under the manual's final-state criteria. Cargo ship bay floors initially retain cargo but move at the end of sandstorm; this creates a trap for naive initial-phase scoring arithmetic. Null hatches permit retained cargo without their own 2-point panel credit, and their placement/preload rules must be considered separately. Do not add an actual panel's 2 points again when cargo goes into that bay.

The cargo is a roughly 13-inch inflatable rubber ball, with inflation, seams, wear and compliance affecting capture. A hatch is a thin, broad disk with a center opening and hook/loop scoring interface. Its planar alignment and release requirements are unlike ball ejection. G4 limits control to one game piece total, including control through other objects. A piece at least partly supported by a rocket or cargo ship is excluded from robot control; this is not permission to carry a hatch and a cargo together. Read the examples on physical pages 53–54 before planning acquisition transitions. This edition does not recommend a bulk-storage architecture for this game.

## Separate ranking architecture from playoff route selection

A complete rocket contains 30 piece points: `6×2 + 6×3`. The RP can be strategically valuable beyond those 30 points because qualification ranking determines alliance selection leverage. A robot aiming to own that condition needs both piece types and all three levels, plus access to both faces. It must deliver a complete sequence in the available time while defending against drops, travel variance and disruption. “Can reach level 3” is a geometric capability, not a solo-rocket demonstration.

The HAB RP has an especially important dependency boundary. A single level-3 climb earns 12, below the threshold of 15. One partner reaching level 1 supplies the remaining 3, or a partner-carry mechanism can own more of the mechanical task. A robot that climbs level 3 alone cannot truthfully claim to solo HAB Docking with no other robot involved. A partner lift replaces a demanding partner climb with docking, weight, geometry and coordination requirements; it does not make the partner cease to exist.

For 1833, independent completion of the rocket is an architectural objective worth evaluating, while unavoidable partner conditions should be quantified instead of wished away. A lower-level cargo-only robot can be effective in an alliance, but it is not an acceptable default support-only design for this team. Conversely, a primary robot may choose fast low cargo cycles during a playoff match once its partners and target allocation are known. Capability and match assignment are different decisions.

Playoffs remove bonus RP. If a low cargo-ship cycle is faster and produces the same 3 cargo points as a high rocket cycle, high reach earns value only through target availability, traffic, partner allocation or other match-specific conditions. It is incorrect to preserve a qualification rocket-filling schedule mechanically once the objective becomes winning a known playoff matchup. The design should retain broad primary capability while the coach exploits the highest marginal route at that moment.

## Work backwards from the complete task graph

Represent every rocket bay as `unpanelled → panel retained → cargo retained`. A panel-placement failure can force a retry before any cargo cycle has value. A dropped panel or cargo may change the source choice, require floor recovery, occupy legal possession, or make a planned route unavailable. One successful practice cycle says little about the probability of completing twelve ordered placements under defense.

A simple illustrative completion model is `Pcomplete = ∏ pi` only when the individual task probabilities are independent and there is no retry time. Real matches violate both assumptions. A more useful time budget includes scheduled retries and interruption: `Tremaining ≥ Σ expected task times + travel between distinct source/goal states + recovery reserve + climb`. State whether a recovery allowance is in every cycle or charged only on failures. Never quietly multiply an ideal fastest cycle by twelve.

The rocket has two faces and three levels. The lowest hatch center is 19 inches, with 28-inch vertical separation to the next levels, per the field description. These are panel-center references, not universal cargo release heights. A useful workspace includes approach standoff, bumper offsets, panel compliance, the path to loading-station height and collision clearance through every transition. An elevator that passes a ball through its uprights must clear the true compressed and off-center ball, the end effector and cable path, not just a nominal sphere in a single CAD pose.

At a loading station, firm panel retention and clean release often matter more than an elaborate wrist. The gripper must tolerate the panel's source pose, hold during acceleration/impacts, align the scoring plane, transfer to hook/loop, and detach without pulling the panel off again. Expanding fingers through the center opening, compliant wheel gripping, and suction solve different interfaces. They should be compared using approach tolerance, retained load, release delay, recoverability, power, sensing and service burden.

## Legal defense and height limits change reachable routes

G9 allows no more than one robot at or beyond the opponent's cargo-ship line, subject to its forced-crossing exception. G10 limits extension beyond the frame while a robot is completely beyond that line, and G11 limits throwing cargo there. These are spatially defined conditions, not a blanket statement that only one robot anywhere may make defensive contact. G3 prevents crossing the opponent's cargo-ship line during sandstorm. A route model should include where a defender can legally engage and how its own mechanisms must be stowed (physical pages 53–55).

G5 prohibits removing opponents' scored pieces, with a rocket-RP consequence in specified circumstances; the game is not a general descore contest. G13 protects opponents fully inside their HAB zone, with additional climb-credit consequences late in the match. G12 limits robot height in either HAB zone to the alliance-wall height, and G24 limits extension beyond the frame. An architecture needs legal folded and extended states through the route, not only a reachable scoring pose (physical pages 54–59). These boundaries are important when deciding whether high reach, a long arm or a particular climb can coexist with quick travel.

## Handoff versus carrying the ground intake

A chassis-mounted floor collector plus a smaller elevator gripper can reduce moving mass. The collector crosses the bumper, captures cargo and hands it into the scoring mechanism. The extra transfer creates a new failure state, so the net cycle improvement is not automatic. If the lift gripper and collector overlap in motion, the robot needs sensing and interlocks to distinguish “captured,” “ready for handoff,” “transferring” and “secure to raise.” A ball pinched between the mechanisms can cause a false success or jam.

1610 explicitly chose this trade: more total mechanism weight in exchange for less traveling up the elevator. Its binder also describes substantial lift weight reduction from thinner and smaller tubing. Neither observation proves an arbitrary thin tube is stiff enough, or that a ground collector should always be separate. Arm bending, bearing spacing, off-center loads, deceleration and retained-piece shock still determine the acceptable structure.

971's 2019 slides show a floor roller and a compact suction wrist, a lightweight carbon-fiber lift, and a small drivebase. The slides are visual and textual evidence; they are not a narrated transcript. Vacuum grip depends on leak flow, cup conformity, contact load and release behavior. It is not enough to multiply atmospheric pressure by a nominal cup area and call that the defended retention force. A cup on a curved ball can peel under moment even when a flat-plate axial calculation looks generous.

Team 125's [detailed rejected-prototype account](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/suction-and-vacuum/368009/13) is valuable negative evidence. Its team author describes promising hatch handling but concerns about cargo retention under collisions and floor-loader handoff. The team moved to wheels. This does not prove suction was bad on every robot; it shows why the whole acquisition-to-score sequence and defended loading cases must determine a choice.

## The no-turret transfer boundary

254's Backlash used a yawing superstructure in addition to lift, shoulder and wrist. The binder ties that choice to reducing chassis turns and scoring while managing defense. Preserve that historical reason accurately. It does not override 1833's prohibition on turrets. The useful extraction may be a compact dual-piece end effector, drive-mounted motors, joint counterbalance or collision sequencing. A current design must solve heading and approach using chassis motion or another permitted arrangement, and account for the resulting cycle time.

Removing one axis from a historical robot is not a harmless edit. It changes reachable poses, target-facing time, intake presentation and legal perimeter transitions. Compare a fixed-plane pass-through elevator/wrist with a fixed-plane articulated arm using the same source/goal sequence and defended routes. A visually simpler assembly may still require more driver alignment or slower transitions. A visually complex historical robot may contain one excellent simple subsystem worth borrowing without copying the whole machine.

## Documented seasonal development

254's [2019 binder](https://media.team254.com/resources/Team_254_Tech_Binder_2019.pdf), physical pages 9–12 and 20–30, supplies explicit evolution rather than a timeless final snapshot. It describes a shorter pre-Champs arm and larger regional intake, then a longer, lighter arm and smaller championship intake. The team links the changes to torque/current problems, reach and swept volume. Its earlier stinger climb loaded the arm/wrist, while the later suction climb used a different reaction path and elevator power; some brakes consequently became unnecessary. Do not combine the former climber's load assumptions with the final intake and claim that hybrid competed.

The same source describes sandstorm moving from substantial driver involvement toward a tuned autonomous routine by championship. The strategic implication is a reduction in acquisition/alignment variance and driver camera burden, not a bonus multiplier. An agent should compare actual time and reliability, and distinguish a desired routine from one used in competition.

2910's [CAD discussion](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/2910-cad-release-2019/357461) records motor protection after early contact damage and later HAB support changes developed for sharing platform space. It is evidence of design revision and packaging tradeoffs. Its [championship report](https://frcteam2910.org/2019/04/25/2019-world-championships/) also documents a qualification seed below its ambition and an early playoff exit. A high-quality robot is not immune to schedule, failures or ranking variance. Do not misidentify 2910 as the 2019 Houston championship winner because of a practice climb with 1323.

Citrus's 2019 CAD release explicitly says a modeled elevator brake was scrapped and never installed on the competition robot. Its published robot page describes scoring both piece types on all levels and lifting two partners. These illustrate two different kinds of evidence: a competition-capability report and a precise disconfirmation of a CAD feature. Neither grants an exhaustive chronology of every climb mechanism revision or an independently verified “only team” claim.

Houston and Detroit must remain separate. [Houston Einstein](https://www.thebluealliance.com/event/2019cmptx) includes 1323/973/5026 and 254's different alliance. [Detroit Einstein](https://www.thebluealliance.com/event/2019cmpmi) is a separate event with 3707/217/4481 in its winning final series. Scores and rosters establish outcomes, not how each robot scored or defended. This expansion supplies no new match-by-match visual audit or validated causal account of all Einstein tactics.

## Endgame is space as well as points

HAB level 3's physical footprint makes coexistence important. A robot that occupies less top surface can preserve another robot's scoring opportunity, sometimes more effectively than physically lifting it. 254 explored suction at an edge; 2910 documented supports that kept bumpers legally above the level while hanging more of the robot off. The design variable is the complete support polygon and load path, not the label “climb.”

The incremental value of level 3 instead of level 2 is 6 points. That equals two retained cargo in simple scoring arithmetic, but whether those cargo can actually be delivered in the saved time depends on remaining bays, panel state, routes, defense and the reliability of the alternate climb. Similarly, leaving 10 seconds early for a climb may forfeit a complete panel/cargo cycle or almost nothing if targets are saturated. Do not import the 2026 conclusion that climbing was low priority into this game's bonus structure.

For 1833, historical pneumatic kicks, brakes and deploying cylinders remain disallowed as recommended actuation. Adapt the geometry with an electric or justified passive mechanism, then redo holding, release, load transfer, hard stops, failure recovery and legal support checks. An electrically driven vacuum pump is a separate historical pressure system and is not automatic permission to introduce a pneumatic baseline.

## How to use this chapter for a genuinely new game

Begin with a state graph of legal piece transformations and target occupancy, identify the requirements the robot can truly own, and write a timed weak-partner scenario. Then compare acquisition, retained pose, reach, transition count, electrical burden and serviceability. Use the [transfer guide](HISTORICAL_2018_2019_TRANSFER.md) and complete robot packets to retrieve mechanisms by function. A game with boxes and multiple heights may share acquisition mechanics with 2018 and workspaces with 2019 or 2023, while having entirely different scoring incentives. The historical label must never substitute for reading the new manual.
