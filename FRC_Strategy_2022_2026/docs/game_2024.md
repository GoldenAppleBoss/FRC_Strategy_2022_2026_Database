# 2024 — CRESCENDO

CRESCENDO is a supply-and-amplification game with single-note handling and a contested autonomous inventory. The strategic unit is a coordinated stream of notes reaching useful scoring locations, with amplification multiplying the value of the right shots at the right times.

The [official manual](https://firstfrc.blob.core.windows.net/frc2024/Manual/HTML/2024GameManual.htm) values autonomous amp/speaker notes at 2/5 and ordinary teleop amp/speaker notes at 1/2; amplified speaker notes earn 5. Leave is 2. Stage scoring includes onstage, spotlight, harmony and trap components. Melody thresholds increased for district championships and Worlds: ordinary-event 18 notes (15 with cooperation), DCMP 21/18 and Worlds 25/21. A late manual threshold must not be applied retroactively to every March event.

## Core strategic model

Five centerline notes and the alliances' wing notes give autonomous a spatial scarcity problem. Starting routes, pickup reach and partner compatibility decide who can exploit that inventory. A six-note-looking individual routine that blocks a partner can lower alliance output. Autonomous should be planned as a joint allocation of notes, positions and crossing times.

In teleop, two amp deliveries enable a limited amplified interval. The optimal policy depends on how quickly the alliance can supply the amp and use its speaker opportunities. A rapid speaker with no reliable amp partner may spend much of its time taking lower-value shots. Conversely, a dedicated amp/feeder can unlock substantial value while scoring few personal speaker points. The scoring database therefore needs both direct and enabling contributions.

Passing changes the division of labor. One robot can collect near source and deliver across the stage while another remains near the speaker/amp. That reduces full-field travel by the high-throughput scorer, but introduces receiving errors, travel time and a new opportunity for interception. A pass is useful when it replaces more expensive motion; its value cannot be inferred from throw distance alone. The Einstein captions include multiple explicit two-feeder arrangements.

Shooting architecture affects where the robot can receive and how easily it continues scoring under pressure. A compact fixed-heading scorer may be simpler and robust. A turret decouples aiming from collection heading but consumes packaging space and adds wiring/angle limits. An elevator-mounted shooter can vary release height and contribute to trap geometry but moves mass. Compare their complete defended cycle, not only shot range.

## Season development and contrasting robots

The curated histories demonstrate different ways teams reached competitive output. 4414 changed from an early turret machine to a substantially rebuilt fixed-heading robot; the initial and rebuilt configuration must not be merged. 254's turret/spindexer architecture combined acquisition and aiming flexibility with its own shooter revisions. 1690's compact Doppler packaging preserved low mass and maneuverability while its feed path received further anti-jam changes. 5940 used a shooter/elevator architecture that also participates in endgame and trap positioning. These examples show several successful packages, not one mechanism ranking.

The broad progression supported by the source set is from proving local collection/scoring to optimizing note access, autonomous contention, amplification timing and defense-resistant supply. That is an analyst synthesis of the selected robots and matches; the corpus is deliberately biased toward high-EPA, well-published teams and is not a census of all FRC play.

At the highest level, defense can attack an alliance's supply rather than its principal shooter. The [first-hand finals analysis](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/analysis-of-2024-einstein-finals/465032) describes 321 slowing 1323 in final 1 and compares interrupted offense in final 2. It supports treating Newton as a coupled supply/scoring system. The two recorded finals also illustrate that penalties can reverse a small clean-score advantage; the final margin does not directly measure relative launcher quality.

Elsewhere on Einstein, the reviewed commentary describes 9072 defending while also delivering to 4481, and both sides using dual feeders in match 3. Such arrangements redefine what makes a valuable third robot. A machine that cannot match the top scorers' autonomous or aim may still provide timely notes, isolate an opponent, then contribute a reliable endgame. Its required intake and passing mechanisms follow from that role.

## Endgame and practical architecture selection

Trap scoring must be evaluated together with the climb and departure time. The robot may need to hold or acquire a note, position against the chain, avoid partners and complete a precise final transfer. Stage components are not independent additions to the match's maximum: attempts consume time and may conflict with alliance geometry. A trap prototype without a reliable climb should not be recorded as a five-point endgame capability.

For PCH week 3, a useful architecture brief emphasizes immediate possession, consistent automatic shots, a dependable amp mode or passing role and an onstage plan. For later DCMP/Worlds play, add compatible centerline contention, robust high-speed acquisition and an explicit alliance amplification workflow. Reserve mechanical and software time for defense rather than assuming open practice-field shot success will persist.

The mechanism inspiration query should be specific: under-bumper acquisition (1771), low-profile turret packaging (1690/254), shooter-on-elevator and trap integration (5940), or complete rebuild history (4414/2974). Each comparison should include service access, energy use, collision behavior and the date that the configuration was actually used. The PCH benchmark table supplies observed event-level output, while the robot dossiers supply candidate means of producing it.


# CRESCENDO: passing turns three robots into one distributed scoring system

The central analytical mistake in CRESCENDO is to evaluate each robot as if it must acquire, cross the field and score every note itself. Once passing is effective, an alliance can distribute acquisition, transport, amplification and finishing. The mechanism question becomes which role a robot can perform reliably and how its interfaces match its partners. The base chapter contains the rules; this supplement develops that alliance model.

## An intake can be valuable far from the goal

A supplier’s useful output is not necessarily its own speaker score. It can shorten the cleaner’s travel, create an easier collection pattern or keep another robot away from contested traffic. But a note launched toward the right half of the field is not automatically a useful pass. The recipient must see, reach, acquire and stage it without spending more time than a direct cycle would have required.

Record pass arrival location, orientation, bounce, visibility, congestion and the recipient’s readiness. A low, predictable pass can be worth more than a faster shot with a broad landing distribution. A second supplier can increase volume while making the cleaner’s collection less efficient if both deliver into the same space. The correct capacity comparison is between the delivery process and the recipient’s actual acquisition/scoring process, not two independent best-case rate claims.

This is why a famous shooter’s geometry should not be copied into a supplier without considering its range and landing requirements. A mechanism tuned for a controlled speaker trajectory can produce a poor floor supply pattern. Likewise a broad collector that works well on stationary notes may be less tolerant of passes arriving while the chassis moves. These are interface questions that the agent should make explicit.

## Amplification creates scheduled demand

An amplification opportunity makes a note’s timing matter. A supplier can appear idle while it is deliberately staging a note for a coordinated window, or can look productive while delivering notes the cleaner cannot use before the window ends. A high-level architecture brief should therefore include synchronization and staging capacity, not only nominal cycles.

There are several sources of lost opportunity: waiting for the amp action, taking a poor shot because a window is expiring, chasing a note that arrived in an inconvenient place, or having two robots attempt to use the same route. Software, drive-team calls and physical feed reliability all participate. An agent should distinguish a mechanism’s maximum firing rate from the alliance’s rate of producing high-value shots at the right times.

The database should also keep the team’s chosen role separate from its installed capability. A robot may have a speaker shooter and spend an important match supplying or amplifying because partners make that allocation better. Low personal score in that match is not evidence that its shooter failed, nor proof that the shooter was wasted mass. It may preserve a fallback or permit a lineup change against another opponent.

## Defense moves upstream when the scoring end is difficult to stop

If a cleaner can score from useful protected or hard-to-reach positions, pushing it while it shoots may have limited value. A defender can instead attack the suppliers, the transit corridors or the arrival area. This can force a long detour, desynchronize amplification or leave the cleaner without a reachable note. Conversely, an alliance with enough supply margin may tolerate one supplier being slowed and keep scoring nearly normally.

RoboReplay’s 2024 retrospective contrasts a double-passing alliance with a cleaner, supplier and hybrid defender/poacher. It discusses route pressure, switching targets and a late amplification burst. The review does not prove a fixed number of denied points; it shows why the remaining supply chain must be considered when assigning defense. See `IGlyCDDcFlE` in the review ledger. The source’s exact match context should be checked before transferring its tactical conclusions to a PCH event.

A defender’s presence can matter without continuous contact if it removes the short route. But a defender standing near a route that the opponent no longer needs can contribute almost nothing. An agent should compare the opponent’s best response: can it move the passing location, change which supplier uses which corridor, use another scoring pose or switch roles? A tactic that only works while the opponent repeats the original path is a weak basis for a season architecture.

## Autonomous is a resource contest as well as a score

Neutral notes can make the first route choice affect both alliances. Acquiring a note first can deny an opponent its expected autonomous sequence, while displacing a note can change later pickup reliability even if it is not scored immediately. These effects are more complex than adding each robot’s independent autonomous EPA.

The design implication is to retain a reliable way to detect a missed or displaced note and recover to something useful. A fast routine with no recovery can collapse after the first interference. A slower route that reaches less-contested supply can produce more reliable alliance value. The base robot dossiers include examples of missed-note fallback and motion-aware control; retrieve their dated sources rather than assuming every robot used the same software policy.

The 1678 history is also useful because it includes an early scoring machine, a more complicated unsuccessful concept and a later simpler package. Its post-event intake changes address impact survival. That record is evidence that an early autonomous demonstration does not validate every architectural addition, and that collection geometry can be revised after real competition loads expose a weakness. Follow the original interview from the dossier for the specific construction.

## Trap, stage and serviceability have different kinds of value

An endgame task can provide qualification value, alliance score, lineup flexibility or a reason a specialist is selected. Those benefits should be evaluated separately. A mechanism that occupies useful intake or shooter space may be justified at a particular district stage and less important in another role; an agent should not declare it mandatory or worthless from the final champion alone.

Stage access also creates partner and timing constraints. A robot needs a reachable position, an approach that survives traffic and a completion time that leaves some recovery margin. A mechanism that succeeds in an isolated pit demonstration can lose much of its practical value if it needs the same route or space as both partners at the same moment.

Repeated note handling and field contact make serviceability a scoring concern. A replaceable intake side plate or accessible transfer roller can shorten a repair enough to protect several later matches. The right architecture comparison includes the likely failures, spare strategy and repair time between matches. It should not assign zero cost to a complicated mechanism simply because a world-class team kept it working.

## A PCH-to-Worlds development path

Use the historical PCH bands to establish what an alliance actually produced at the relevant stage, then decide which contribution your team can own. A reliable supplier with compatible autonomous and endgame may add more to a strong alliance than a mediocre attempt to match the captain’s cleaner role. But that recommendation requires enough capable finishers in the field and a demonstrated pass-receive interface. If those partners are absent, the same specialist can become supply-rich and score-poor.

At higher levels, evaluate lineup flexibility and counterplay. The agent should be able to explain why a mechanism is useful in a distributed scoring system, which partner properties it assumes and what happens if the alliance loses a supplier or a cleaner. Those assumptions are the durable context to carry into a later shooter game. The note shape, protection zones, storage limits and amplification rules do not transfer automatically.
