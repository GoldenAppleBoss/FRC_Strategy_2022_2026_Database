# 2025 — REEFSCAPE

REEFSCAPE is a parallel alliance-work problem: finite upper branches, repeatable L1 placement, contested algae and shared supply lanes. At elite level, a highly capable robot can maximize alliance score by specializing in L1 and algae throughout teleop while its partners cover branches.

## Corrected high-end model: complementary jobs from the start

An elite REEFSCAPE alliance should be designed as three coordinated work streams, not three copies of the highest branch scorer. The user's concrete example is Newton's 2025 alliance: 1323 spent its teleoperated work on L1 and algae while 2910 and 4272 handled the higher reef, then joined L1. The official/stored finals lineups establish 1323, 2910 and 4272 as the three appearing robots; 5026 was the alliance's reserve. The user's assessment that 1323 was the best robot is an informed preference, not an objective title inferred from EPA.

The exact all-match task timing in that example is preserved as the user's observation; this database has not completed a visual audit of every match. It is consistent with 1323's published L1-capable mechanism package, the reviewed team/interview context and the championship commentary's discussion of upper-reef completion and L1 transitions. That combination is enough to correct the architecture model, while keeping the evidence boundaries visible. Do not turn it into a fabricated second-by-second scouting ledger or assume every Newton match assigned precisely the same actions.

The important strategic point is stronger than “L1 becomes useful after the reef is full.” A robot can rationally work L1 from the beginning of teleop because its partners already provide enough branch throughput. Parallel work avoids delaying low-level placement and algae security until the expensive robots finish everything else. The goal is to maximize the alliance's final useful score and resilience, not to maximize each robot's average coral height. A fast all-level robot may be the best L1/algae specialist precisely because its acquisition, orientation, control and driving are excellent.

Consider a hypothetical alliance with two robots that will cover the remaining upper branches on schedule. A third upper-branch cycle can consume a target, occupy a face, cross a supply lane and force a partner to select another task. Its marginal gain can be smaller than its nominal point value. A deliberate L1 placement may add independent work and preserve the other two robots' rhythm. If that third robot can also secure an algae that would otherwise be contested, the value includes the opponent's lost opportunity. These are conditional comparisons; actual partner rates, misses, penalties and traffic must be measured rather than assumed from season EPA.

## Why 1323's mechanism package matters to that role

[1323's binder](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Y2ooTZMBNoViMZ6R4wYOkom1mhIIqF-R/view?usp=sharing) documents a ground coral collector, separate algae contact, their combined L1 function, a compact multi-stage lift and a differential end effector. The detailed mechanism cards preserve page references and selected reductions. The construction supports a broader interpretation: L1 is a precision manipulation task with different geometry, not a lower setting on an L4 elevator. Orienting coral horizontally, constraining it during release, approaching an occupied trough and recovering algae without wasting a long cycle all affect alliance output.

The [team's detailed intake reply](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/500435/131) makes the intention explicit: horizontal L1 packing was a high-level objective, while the separate centering/indexing approach appeared difficult and insufficiently robust in their prototypes. The same reply describes troublesome pickup angles and considerable practice. The successful package therefore combines a strategic placement objective, a particular contact solution and execution work; it should not be described as a trivially omnidirectional floor intake.

The shared-axis intakes are especially important to retrieve together. An algae guide can also help define a coral release boundary, while separate deployment coordinates create the desired relative geometry. A differential wrist and independent roller contacts can orient the tube before placement. Those functions can justify a technically sophisticated robot even when its visible teleop work occurs low to the carpet. An agent that equates high reach with high quality will miss this value and may recommend an unnecessarily tall or slow package for the actual alliance job.

The same robot's reach and climb capabilities still matter as flexibility and contingency. If a partner fails, a branch is left empty, the opponent changes algae access, or an autonomous misses, the assigned job can change. A specialized match role does not imply a robot incapable of other work. The correct record therefore has separate fields for physical capability, documented configuration, assigned role and observed execution. “1323 did L1” should never erase its other mechanisms, and a full-featured CAD model should never imply it used every feature equally in the finals.

## How that changes a kickoff and rebuild brief

Define at least three plausible partner scenarios before ranking tasks. One should represent a PCH alliance still limited by branch completion; another should represent strong partners covering most branches; a third should include a disabled or unreliable partner. Compare an all-level scorer, a fast L1/algae package and a simpler support robot against the same scenarios. Autonomous contribution, source compatibility, human-player supply and deep-climb reliability remain separate terms. The selected architecture should state which scenario it targets and which fallback it preserves.

For a team that can realistically captain an elite alliance, L1 speed and packing can be core requirements from kickoff, not a late accessory. For a PCH week-3 team whose partners cannot fill branches, upper-level scoring may retain more marginal value. Both can be true without assigning permanent value to a scoring height. The historical event benchmarks help define the competition environment; they do not replace a model of the remaining tasks and the partners who will do them.

When recommending inspiration, retrieve a whole task chain: supply, capture envelope, orientation, controlled release, recovery and route back to the next piece. For L1, explicitly test placement into an already occupied region and interaction with partner approaches. For algae, distinguish reef removal, carpet acquisition, secure transport and the eventual scoring policy. The winning architecture is the one that makes the alliance's intended division of labor repeatable under disruption. That is the strategic context the technical database now places before individual mechanism comparisons.

The [official manual](https://firstfrc.blob.core.windows.net/frc2025/Manual/HTML/2025GameManual.htm) values L1/L2/L3/L4 coral at 3/4/6/7 in autonomous and 2/3/4/5 in teleop; processor algae is 6 and net algae 4. Leave is 3, park/shallow/deep are 2/6/12. Wins award 3 ranking points. The dated 254 binder records the early five-per-level coral bonus; the later manual contains increased requirements. Keep rule versions attached to event targets.

## Core demands

There are 12 branches on each of L2–L4. Upper-branch capacity is finite, so L1 and algae can deserve a dedicated robot from the beginning of teleop when the partners already cover that work; the specialist need not wait for literal saturation. Once all 36 positions are filled, further ordinary branch cycling adds no score. This creates two different robot-selection problems: becoming the fastest early branch scorer, and adding output after a strong alliance has already saturated those branches. A robot optimized only for L4 may be excellent at a district event but less differentiated on a saturated elite alliance.

The unprotected acquisition route makes floor collection strategically consequential. A human player can deliver coral toward the reef, allowing the robot to turn a long station cycle into a short local pickup. But arbitrary pipe orientations make the collector and orientation system difficult: a wide mouth increases the capture envelope while requiring a reliable centering/handoff path. Prototype that path with badly oriented, multiple and rebounding coral, not only a carefully placed pipe.

Bidirectional scoring can remove turns, and low moving mass can permit aggressive driving with the manipulator elevated. These benefits are coupled to stiffness, backlash and calibration. If an elevated arm deflects, vision accuracy alone will not place the coral. The 1690, 2910, 254 and 604 dossiers offer different mechanical responses; they should be compared on repeatable placement time and recovery, not counted degrees of freedom.

Algae has several simultaneous roles: it obstructs some branch access, scores independently, and can be denied to the opponent. Floor pickup, reef removal and retention under contact are different capabilities. A gripper that removes algae from the reef may be unable to acquire one lying on carpet. A processor score also changes access to an algae for the opposing human-player system, so comparing 6 versus 4 without that context can be misleading.

## Evolution and alliance geometry

The reviewed histories show floor intakes being added or extensively rebuilt after early events, including 111's change from station-only acquisition. 4414's second complete robot made a full-width ground intake central to its rebuild. Other teams refined floating rollers, active orientation, handoff sensing and floor vision rather than changing their primary reach mechanism. Those changes target effective cycle time, not simply nominal scoring capability.

As alliances completed reefs more reliably, L1 packing, hybrid coral/algae cycles and net occupancy became more important. 1323's interview explicitly discusses emphasizing L1 at Worlds. This is not evidence that L4 was unimportant earlier; it is evidence that the alliance's bottleneck moved. A high-level planner must predict the other two robots' output and score-state evolution before deciding its own marginal role.

The [1690 Championship retrospective](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/orbit-1690-2025-robot-reveal-whisper/492064/160) provides a valuable counterexample to ground pickup always being better. It reports moving 2073 onto a fixed station lane from Einstein match 8 to stop interference with its faster partners. The same account describes algae acquisition changes and execution failures across the finals. These are first-hand reports, not independently timed measurements.

The lesson is spatial: all three robots cannot simultaneously own the same short pickup lane. A station-fed third robot can raise alliance total by making the two ground-fed routes more predictable. A strategy database should model lane occupancy and human-player supply as shared resources. Selecting three maximum individual EPAs without that model can produce a weaker alliance than the numbers suggest.

The Einstein score breakdowns also help avoid overfitting the winner. Final 1 has equal recorded climb points; a close score cannot be explained by one alliance having a better deep-climb architecture. Later missed climbs, dropped coral and algae acquisition interact. The records preserve those distinctions and keep unverified causal explanations separate from score facts.

## High-level design recommendations

An event-captain brief should derive its task allocation from likely partners and the intended stage of play. Upper-level placement, dedicated L1/algae work, compatible autonomous and acquisition under defense are alternative or overlapping jobs. A robot assigned L1/algae can be the alliance's most capable robot; quality is not ordered by scoring height. A third robot may use a station lane that preserves its partners' faster floor-supply routes. Endgame value must include alignment time and the ability to retain the cage after disable.

Borrow a 2910-style low-pivot extending arm only after checking reach, load distribution, backlash and climb sharing. Borrow a 1690-style lightweight differential/vacuum system only after validating seal reliability, belt life and manufacturing capability. Borrow a 254-style floor orientation system with its whole handoff and failure fallback. A team number identifies a working package under a set of assumptions; it is not a guarantee that one extracted component will work in a different package.

For PCH, use the week-specific event output in the benchmark table. A resource plan should state what must work by the first event and which later gains require a new collector, stronger automation or more practice. The early and late coral-RP thresholds differ; qualify and playoff objectives also differ. The database keeps both distinctions visible so a kickoff agent does not optimize a late-season ranking requirement as if it were a universal scoring law.


# REEFSCAPE: from placing coral to controlling space, time and remaining value

REEFSCAPE is the season with the most complete RoboReplay progression in this edition: kickoff hypotheses, early events, week-by-week adjustments, district championships, division finals, Einstein and IRI are represented in the reviewed transcript set. The review ledger preserves one source-specific account per video. This chapter is an original conditional synthesis, not a claim that one commentator observed every event or that a single tactic caused a championship.

## The first useful model is different from the last useful model

At kickoff, coral provides a large collection of scoring opportunities, algae affects access and endgame offers another source of points and ranking value. A team still needs a robot that can collect, orient, reach, release and recover. A sophisticated saturated-reef policy is useless if the robot cannot repeatedly complete those basic sequences.

As robots and partners improve, a different constraint appears: how much of the remaining reef can the alliance finish, and how quickly can it reach the low-value work left afterward? A robot that seems redundant in an early all-level task list can become valuable by doing a specialized job after partners cover the obvious branches. This is not evidence that every team should have optimized for the final saturated regime at kickoff. It is a reason to set milestones and leave a practical route toward it.

## Floor supply is a joint human-and-robot architecture

The early reviewed examples show human-player coral delivery reducing travel for ground collectors. Later examples show broad supply and techniques that aim coral toward particular work areas. The strategic gain depends on more than a pickup mechanism. It needs delivery, orientation tolerance, acquisition, handoff, scoring and awareness of remaining supply. A robot can lose the apparent gain if it repeatedly stops to chase a poorly placed piece or has a transfer that takes longer than the travel it removed.

Flooding also trades something away. Extra coral distributed across the floor may accelerate convenient cycles while leaving some pieces in awkward, invisible or contested locations. That can be acceptable when the alliance will not exhaust its supply, then become costly near saturation. An agent should track the phase where wasted placement ceases to be harmless. A high-scoring clip does not establish that the same delivery policy remains optimal against theft or with different partners.

Funnel acquisition remains a meaningful counterexample to a simplistic floor-intake rule. The Hopper review discusses 1768’s large funnel, human-player coordination and fast station cycles. The important comparison is complete-cycle performance under defense, including the geometry of the receiving target. A wide passive receiving region can reduce the effect of a bump at the instant of delivery. That does not eliminate the travel penalty, but it can change whether the added complexity of a ground system is worthwhile for a particular team.

## Autonomous compatibility can create a third useful scorer

Two stations and several robots impose a joint schedule. First-branch selection, stagger timing, bumper starting pose and ground-preplaced coral can change whether partners collide or wait. The review progression includes center algae routines and later backside/lollipop routines that leave other routes usable. Do not assume that the first team a commentator noticed invented the concept; several videos explicitly disclaim that knowledge.

The lasting value of another autonomous coral also depends on the final state. If both alliances eventually fill a branch, the autonomous premium and the teleop time released are the relevant benefits. Counting the entire autonomous score again as an independent end-of-match advantage double-counts the ordinary placement. If one alliance does not fill the branch, the comparison changes. This is why a strategic agent should simulate the remaining work, not just add phase EPA values as if the tasks were unrelated.

## L1 geometry changes where scoring can continue

Once higher branches are reliably covered by partners, fast L1 work becomes an alliance-specific opportunity. A controlled horizontal release can support a different packing pattern from an upright drop. That can postpone travel to the far side, improve visibility and reduce the chance of disturbing existing coral. The value is not merely another two points per piece: it is the additional useful placement available near the current work area, minus the stability and recovery cost.

The 1323 deep note explains why this specialization must be read with its partners and controls. The finals transcript also describes disturbed stacks and interprets their effect. A denser stack is not free capacity if a partner’s approach can remove several pieces. The design brief should therefore include placement tolerance, partner clearance, resistance to incidental contact and the cost of re-scoring fallen pieces.

## Algae can be worth a swing, but the swing is conditional

Taking a piece the opponent would otherwise score and successfully scoring it yourself can both deny their points and add your own. That is the origin of the often-discussed eight-point swing for a four-point net score. It is not an unconditional value assigned to every algae pickup. If the opponent would never reach it, if you miss, if recovering it consumes valuable coral time or if the theft incurs penalties, the actual value is lower and can be negative.

The reviewed progression includes 2910’s steals, quick algae security and 2767’s autonomous counter. It also includes Curie and IRI examples where an incursion or attempted rescue becomes vulnerable to containment. These are useful opposing cases. An agent should retrieve both a successful theft and a punished theft before recommending the capability as a season goal. Escape route, visibility, opponent reaction and what the other two partners can do are part of the design context.

## Processor policy depends on the opponent, not only the score label

The processor gives one alliance points while offering the opponent a human-player scoring opportunity. The expected differential therefore depends on the opponent’s success probability, time, crowding, awareness and whether the same algae can be recovered. RoboReplay’s Michigan examples argue for a high-variance upset policy; later videos describe improved human-player technique and direct processor defense. The hypothesized crowding threshold is not a universal probability model.

A specialist can thus change value sharply between matches. It may dominate against an opponent that ignores its processor, then contribute little when access is defended. A robot that also has a net shot, a useful autonomous or a defensive fallback has more ways to preserve value. The appropriate architecture recommendation should name that contingency rather than promise that one low-complexity scoring function will always earn a playoff role.

## Defense is a time-and-space policy, not a pushing statistic

The PCH 7538 discussion emphasizes acquisition disruption, switching targets and sustained flat-bumper contact. Other reviews show how ground supply, algae/coral role switching, visibility and protected scoring regions limit a defender. The relevant observation is not how hard one robot hit another. It is how much useful work the opponent lost, what work the defender surrendered and whether penalties or a partner’s opportunity offset the gain.

A narrow station-blocking tactic can become ineffective if the opponent has enough supply from another location. A less visibly aggressive zone defender may be useful by forcing a longer path. A high-scoring captain can rationally defend if its partners retain the better remaining matchup. Those conclusions require the actual lineups and routes. They should not be inferred from the robot’s EPA or from the commentator’s praise alone.

## Drafting, endgame and evidence boundaries

The division reviews show why field depth, invitation/decline order and the value of the third robot matter. A low seed can obtain a strong lineup in a deep field; that does not establish a universal rule to decline a top invitation. Endgame decisions also depend on what remains to score, travel and the reliability of all three climbs. An early departure can surrender several useful cycles; an overly late one can lose the climb. Use a distribution, not one successful last-second example.

For PCH, use the district’s own stage benchmarks rather than New England point examples from a video. For Worlds, use division-specific lineups and actual match records. Keep IRI separate because its rules and selection format differed. The durable lesson is that the highest-value task changes with the alliance’s remaining work and the opponent’s response. The mechanism database becomes useful when it records those conditions beside the attractive architecture.
