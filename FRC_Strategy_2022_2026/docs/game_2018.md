# 2018 — FIRST POWER UP

## Read the game as control of time and access

POWER UP is a one-piece-possession game about establishing and preserving ownership of a shared scale and an alliance-specific switch. A cube is an investment in future scoring time, not a fixed number of points. The best action depends on the current balance, expected remaining ownership, where the next available cube is, and what the opponent can do while you travel. This makes a raw cubes-per-match ranking an incomplete description of robot value.

The governing source for the rules in this chapter is the [official 133-page manual](https://firstfrc.blob.core.windows.net/frc2018/Manual/2018FRCGameSeasonManual.pdf), especially physical PDF pages 33, 37–42 and 51–59. Section revision labels differ within the assembled PDF; do not call it an unchanging kickoff ruleset. This chapter is a retrospective architecture study, not a substitute for checking team updates or Q&A for a particular event. Numeric examples below are calculations or hypothetical scenarios, not reconstructed match observations.

A match has 15 seconds of autonomous play and 135 seconds of teleop, with the last 30 seconds designated endgame. Autonomous ownership is worth an initial 2 points and 2 per additional second; teleop ownership is worth an initial 1 and 1 per additional second, subject to powerups. Each autonomous line crossing earns 5 points. Each cube placed in the vault earns 5 points. Parking is 5 and climbing is 30 per qualifying robot. Read the exact support and bumper criteria before specifying a climb.

The Power Cube is not a 2023 foam cube. The manual describes a nylon-covered HDPE crate approximately 13 by 13 by 11 inches and 3.25 pounds. Its open fabric-covered face, rigid edges, seams and orientation change how rollers engage. Under G22 the robot may control only one cube. An acquisition mechanism should therefore maximize rapid ownership, pose tolerance and recovery, rather than accumulate a hopper. A retained cube can be reoriented by a wrist or gripper, but a robot cannot solve a long route with bulk storage.

## What can and cannot be independently owned

AUTO QUEST requires all three robots to complete AUTO-RUN and the alliance to own its switch at the end of auto. One robot can own the switch requirement and its own movement, but cannot directly cause a disabled partner's line crossing. FACE THE BOSS requires three physical climbs, or two physical climbs plus LEVITATE. A robot that can carry one partner and supply LEVITATE cubes owns much of the engineering burden, while retaining partner mounting and human-player execution dependencies. Record those residual dependencies explicitly. Do not label either entire condition guaranteed solo RP.

For 1833, the transferable objective is a primary robot that can win the scale race, protect its switch and complete the attainable bonus work with weak partners. It is not a switch-only support design. Historical specialists remain useful mechanism references and counterexamples. A primary robot can spend a particular playoff match on an opposing switch without having been designed with a low capability ceiling.

The two switches are independent. Scoring on the opponent's switch denies their ownership points; it does not make that switch score for you. Scale ownership, by contrast, is contested by both alliances. In a hypothetical 20-second teleop window, changing the scale from opponent-owned to yours can improve the relative score by roughly 40 recurring points, before acquisition-transition details and powerups. Merely stopping their switch for the same window denies roughly 20 recurring points. These are conditional marginal values, not a permanent 2:1 rule: a scale you cannot hold may be a worse investment than a short, repeatable switch attack.

Likewise, an extra cube on a securely owned scale has little immediate scoring value but can buy reserve against an opposing cycle. The useful question is how many seconds of future ownership it protects, and at what opportunity cost. A cube added to an already hopelessly lost scale can have effectively zero ownership return. The agent must calculate the catch-up deficit, available inventory, competing cycle rates, and remaining time before recommending another scale cycle.

## Spatial economy and why acquisition changed the ceiling

The finite field supply begins in fence rows, portals and protected pyramids. Early accessible cubes are different from late portal cycles. A mechanism that cleanly removes one pyramid cube without scattering the others preserves access and can protect the following autonomous acquisition. A pickup that succeeds eventually but disorganizes the inventory may lower the entire alliance's subsequent throughput. Any claimed four-cube autonomous depends on both path execution and the state left by the previous pickup.

A full cycle includes approach, capture, confirmation, reorientation, transport, lift, placement and escape. Some motions overlap: raising an elevator while driving can save time, but it changes stability and may require acceleration limits. Front/back scoring can remove a chassis turn; it also requires a collision-free pass-through and controlled retention during rotation. A mechanism is valuable when that saved turn survives the full cycle, not because the wrist can spin impressively on a stand.

An elevator's maximum reach alone is insufficient. Scale height changes with balance, and accumulated cubes make the target a pile rather than a flat empty pan. The robot may need both rapid ejection and accurate placement, especially when a rushed cube can rebound, fall, or destabilize previously scored cubes. Cube orientation affects packing and effective pile height. 148 explicitly designed its boot intake to normalize orientation. A camera-based estimate of scale attitude can reduce unnecessary lift travel, as 254's binder describes, but vision latency and occlusion remain interfaces to handle.

Tangent-to-route acceleration, turn behavior and traversal over the scale platform matter more than unconstrained top speed. A tall loaded elevator raises overturning moment; a mechanism advertised as fast may force the driver to brake early or wait to lower before accelerating. Chassis wheel placement, lift setback, battery position, deployed forks, and a partner's mass during climbing must be examined as a common load system. Do not copy a drive gearing value without the original wheel size, motor assumptions and acceleration policy.

## Powerups: a scheduling and opportunity-cost problem

LEVITATE costs three vault cubes and credits one climb up to the alliance maximum. Those cubes also earn their normal vault points. It therefore offers a different package of value from simply comparing 30 climb points with 15 vault points. FORCE grants ownership for 10 seconds: one cube targets the switch, two the scale, three both. BOOST doubles ownership points for 10 seconds on the analogous targets, but depends on actually earning ownership. Each powerup can be played only once. Additional cubes after activation can still earn vault points under the manual's rules.

The global FORCE/BOOST queue is a subtle constraint: these powerups do not simply overlap freely because different alliances press buttons. A powerup request can wait for the current one to expire. A timing plan must include both alliances' queue and the remaining match time. Never award a full 10-second benefit to a powerup that cannot finish before the match ends, or assume BOOST guarantees ownership while a plate is lost.

The vault has three columns holding up to three cubes each, so it is a finite nine-cube scoring destination rather than an unlimited five-points-per-cube outlet. Playing FORCE or BOOST early at a lower level does not permit replaying or upgrading that powerup later. Additional legal cubes can still fill the column for vault points. Capacity and activation are separate states (manual physical pages 29–30 and 41–42).

There is no universal instruction to fill the vault first. In a contested match, cubes directed to powerups are cubes not establishing a durable scale lead or interrupting the opponent's switch. The marginal comparison changes once a lead is safe, a comeback is impossible, or a powerup can secure a critical final interval. The correct decision is state-dependent, with a human player and drive coach coordinating execution.

## Legal contact and release locations shape the mechanism

Under G09, launching a cube is permitted only in specified relationships to the intended scale plate, switch or exchange: the scale condition uses the alliance's null territory, the switch condition uses fence contact/intersection, and the exchange condition uses the exchange zone. A historical roller's ability to eject quickly is therefore not a general permission for long-range scoring. Approach geometry and legal release position belong in the cycle model (physical pages 53–54).

G16 protects a robot meeting its own null-territory conditions from opposing contact. G18 protects qualifying platform-zone robots during endgame, with a climb-credit consequence. G12 also constrains coordinated shutdown of major gameplay access, while G14 and G15 govern pinning and exchange blocking. The championship account of portal defense must be read within those limits, not as permission to camp anywhere indefinitely or block all supplies with several robots. This shapes which paths can be reliably contested and which targets are protected once approached correctly (physical pages 55–56).

## Documented evolution, with limits on the historical claim

The most concrete before/after evidence in this expansion is mechanism-specific rather than a complete week-by-week census. Team 1678's [2018 CAD release, replies 8 and 12](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/team-1678-citrus-circuits-2018-cad-release/166149) distinguishes its earlier horizontal roller intake from a later side-to-side design adapted from actual 1323 CAD. The later intake was intentionally omitted from the public assembly. This is evidence of design transfer and a revision; it is not permission to invent the missing CAD or claim a measured cycle improvement.

2898's binder separately documents an initial scale-capable elevator robot, failures involving reach/alignment/sensors, and a switch/exchange rebuild that retained the intake and drivebase. That is an instructive recovery under the contemporary bag-and-tag constraints. Its narrowed role is not 1833's desired kickoff objective. The transferable lesson is to detect architecture-interface risks before expensive manufacture, and to preserve useful modules if recovery becomes necessary. “This team rebuilt” does not imply rebuilding is cheap.

At the elite endpoint, [254's championship account](https://blog.team254.com/2018cmp/) describes choosing between scale auto and combined switch/scale auto based on partner confidence. Its Houston playoff plan combined scale ownership, attack on the opponent's switch, and interruption of portal supply. It also reports deliberately limited exchange investment. This is a team account of its own strategy, not a visual audit of every Einstein action. The causal interpretation is that control of inventory access and competing scoring clocks can dominate simply maximizing the number of cubes placed in one target.

254's binder provides an unusually direct chain from first-seed ambition to autonomous output, all-target capability and a partner lift. That chain resembles 1833's wish to control qualification outcomes, while the exact 2018 implementation uses historical pneumatic hardware that must not be copied into a current 1833 recommendation. Do not infer that a no-turret preference means all lift-and-wrist complexity is justified; the saved turns must repay the complete integration burden.

Houston and Detroit were separate championship fields. The [Houston Einstein record](https://www.thebluealliance.com/event/2018cmptx) identifies the 254/148/2976/3075 championship alliance. The [Detroit record](https://www.thebluealliance.com/event/2018cmpmi) is a different tournament and includes Stryke Force's alliance. Treat the winners and match scores as event facts; do not use the Houston team's tactical report as an explanation of Detroit matches. This edition does not add individual visual audits, new PCH benchmarks, or an EPA-selected 25-team cohort for 2018.

The new game must be decomposed before any of these references earns priority. A box-shaped piece does not make the game POWER UP, and tall scoring does not automatically require a multi-stage elevator. Start with scoring states, possession rules, travel distances, target workspace, accuracy, denial and bonus prerequisites. Only then compare coherent mechanisms.

## Useful first-pass calculations

Write `Tcycle = Tacquire + Ttravel + Tplace + Tescape − Toverlap`, with every term defined and a separate recovery allowance. For ownership, estimate the area under the alliance ownership indicator over time rather than points per cube. Keep the probability of a failed pickup, the time cost of recovery and the chance a cube remains scored separate. For lifts, calculate force and power through the actual rigging, spool radius, moving-stage masses and efficiency; a motor count and gear ratio copied from a binder are not a new design calculation.

For a steady forward acceleration, compare overturning moment `m a h` with gravitational restoring moment `m g b`, where `h` is loaded system CG height and `b` is distance from the CG projection to the relevant support edge. This is a screening calculation, not a contact-impact or dynamic proof. In the real robot the cube, moving carriage and any partner alter both terms. A targeted test should address the unresolved boundary that could reverse an architecture choice, after the cheaper envelope and load checks have been done.
