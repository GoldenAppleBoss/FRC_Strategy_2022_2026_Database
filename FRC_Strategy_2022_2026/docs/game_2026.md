# 2026 — REBUILT

REBUILT is a fuel-control and fuel-throughput game. The default competitive architecture prioritizes acquisition, retained inventory, useful scoring, passing/denial, routes and reliability. Climbing was strategically marginal across the sampled season and should receive no default mechanism allocation.

## Corrected architecture priority: fuel, not climbing

The central 2026 design question is how the alliance acquires, controls, retains and scores fuel. Climbing should not appear as a co-equal primary objective merely because the rules contain tower points. The user's correction is consistent with the stored event sample throughout the season, including PCH events before Worlds. The default architecture recommendation is to allocate mass, volume, power, practice and engineering time to useful fuel contribution. A climb needs a specific positive marginal case before it earns those resources.

This distinction matters at kickoff as well as in hindsight. A task inventory says what is legal; it does not say what deserves development effort. A point-value table cannot account for the hopper volume displaced by a climber, the day spent fixing its deployment, or the time the drivers lose practicing it. A calculator that compares only final seconds of scoring with final seconds of climbing is still incomplete. A robot can pay a climb's opportunity cost for the entire season without ever choosing to climb during an important match.

The numerical audit uses actual tower component fields from the stored match payloads, rather than EPA endgame estimates. It excludes missing components instead of converting them to zero. In 2,237 valid Worlds-division alliance performances, 95.4% record zero teleop tower points; the mean is about 0.60. All 32 Einstein alliance performances record zero autonomous and teleop tower points. In the sampled PCH week-3 event, 91.8% of 146 alliance performances record zero teleop tower points; week 5 has 93.3% of 150; DCMP has 89.0% of 210. Week-1, week-2 and week-4 samples likewise show tower scoring as a minor exception. The detailed denominators and missing-field counts are in `data/tower_summary_2026.jsonl`.

These are selected historical event records, not an exhaustive census of every 2026 match. A few nonzero tower scores establish that climbing happened; they do not make it a central architecture priority. Nor does a large value in a field named endgame establish a climb: 2026 endgame also contains substantial fuel scoring. Component EPA can be negative because it is a model estimate, so it cannot serve as a physical climb count. The agent must use the actual tower fields for this distinction.

There is direct technical evidence for the opportunity-cost interpretation. [2046's binder, PDF page 32](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1YBlY312kMTiV29boVv6xRt87foDIU9lk/view?usp=sharing#page=32) says the team dropped a six-pound, ten-point climber that occupied hopper volume. [6800's DCMP binder, PDF page 8](https://drive.google.com/file/d/11rQrMwCIseY18JbKFytGLXSd__x1JP1S/view?usp=sharing#page=8) says its earlier telescope did not compete at districts because of weight constraints and higher-priority scoring work. These are accounts of particular teams' decisions, not an assertion that every robot had the same development path.

## Resource control does not excuse a fuel path that cannot meet its job

Passing, denying supply and preparation for the next shift remain central. The failure mode is to use a plausible alliance strategy as a substitute for integrated performance. [2910's original brief](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1jPf-aOGhz0dntoAgahFBgRhJiJv9rGpj/view?usp=sharing) explains the minimal-hopper, dual-intake passing hypothesis. [The rebuild brief](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1r-6zR4afbr_BpeZfWwcM5XL1N74FjScI/view?usp=sharing) explains why its actual throughput, scoring and partner dependence did not justify that package. The replacement changes storage, pickup kinematics and the entire feeder/scorer interface. It is not simply the same robot with one more lane.

That history should remain available to the agent as a falsified local hypothesis. The original strategy had a coherent resource argument: move fuel to an area where partners can score it. Its weakness was the conjunction of assumptions needed to make that role valuable under real competition conditions. Recommending a similar architecture requires tests of useful delivered fuel, recoverability of the resulting pile, autonomous compatibility, self-scoring when partners fail and performance under denial. It cannot rely on a future partner solving every missing function.

Likewise, the observed spread of drums should not turn into an instruction to maximize lane count. A third or fourth lane can increase the nominal launch ceiling while making indexing, current or structure worse. The mechanism notes document changes to floors, backing, transitions, rigidity and service access because those interfaces determine whether the ceiling is realized. A compact turret can remain strong if it is supplied and accurately firing for more of the usable time. Compare complete operating policies under the same fuel distribution and defense, including the final few balls and depleted battery.

For a PCH rebuild, the resulting goal hierarchy begins with reliable acquisition, retained and deliverable capacity, repeatable useful output, route access, jam recovery and survival. Autonomous and shift-transition preparation belong in that same fuel model. Tower capability is a separate, low-priority exception. This hierarchy is now the default for the agent rather than an optional caution buried after a rules summary.

The [official manual](https://firstfrc.blob.core.windows.net/frc2026/Manual/HTML/2026GameManual.htm) has 20 seconds of autonomous and 140 of teleop: transition, four alternating shifts and a shared endgame. Scored fuel is worth 1; inactive-hub fuel is not. Tower scoring includes autonomous L1 and teleop L1/L2/L3. Current published fuel thresholds vary by event tier, so a kickoff model needs the historical update, not just the newest manual. In the statistical data, endgame points include late fuel as well as tower points.

## Model the complete fuel path

The useful performance metric is scored fuel per active window, not fuel leaving the robot per second on a stand. For a batch scorer, the path contains travel to supply, collection, loaded transit, alignment, spin-up, discharge and residual-fuel recovery. For a turret robot, some of those actions can overlap. The effective choice depends on the amount of overlap that survives defense, route traffic, a changing hopper load and battery limits.

A simple upper bound is `usable fuel ≤ min(fuel acquired and retained, launcher service capacity during available shots)`. That bound is necessary but optimistic: it omits travel, misses and downtime. Capacity can reduce the number of transits, but additional fuel increases loaded mass and can worsen bump traversal, tipping risk and current draw. The relevant storage value is fuel delivered in time, not fuel photographed in the hopper.

Trench access has tactical and autonomous value because it changes the route graph. A low robot can use an additional passage, while a tall bump robot may recover that lost route flexibility through greater capacity or release height. An expanding hopper or flexible net introduces a state-dependent envelope: the robot might fit under the trench empty but not fully loaded. The database therefore distinguishes source-reported capacity and travel configurations instead of assigning one unqualified capacity number.

## Why wide drums spread

The reviewed 254 interview explains choosing parallel fuel streams for a higher possible instantaneous ceiling. Many other teams subsequently rebuilt from single/dual streams to wide drums: 2910, 6329, 1768, 3467 and 6800 provide explicit histories. That pattern is directly supported by their accounts; it is not proof that every turret was a strategic mistake.

The transferable drum architecture includes much more than the large wheel. Fuel must be accelerated predictably before release; the path must preserve usable width, avoid bridging and control compression/spin. A splitter, a motor or a poorly shaped transition can become the true limiter. The 254 dossier describes contact continuity, 2910 describes preloaded path storage, 1678 describes an S-to-J change, and 2046 reports retaining an S-path after its own comparison. These differing results are useful evidence against a universal geometry recipe.

Inertia is similarly conditional. Extra stored energy can reduce speed droop during a dense volley, while adding mass and spin-up load. A low-inertia scorer may change range quickly and rely on control authority to recover. A team should measure launch-speed distribution, motor current, energy over a whole match, spin-up delay and field accuracy. More motors or a heavier drum are not independent guarantees of more points.

## Why turrets remained competitive

A turret decouples aiming from chassis heading, potentially allowing collection, route changes and firing to overlap. Its single-stream limit can be offset by a larger fraction of the usable window spent shooting accurately. That benefit depends on singulation remaining continuous, adequate rotation range, a compact swept envelope and working moving-shot compensation. A turret that is starved, jammed or forced through frequent cable unwinds loses much of that advantage.

4414's RIPCURRENT is an integrated example. Its compact shooter, large low singulator, structural hopper boundaries and extensible storage must be understood together. The published binder reports **85+ fuel under the trench and 100+ with the net over the bump**; the user's 110-fuel statement is not established by this review. The nearby 110-inch number is frame perimeter. DYE Rotor refers to paintball-loader lineage; it does not mean two rotors.

Other references expose different packages: 125 enlarges the rotor and shapes its chassis around it; 1987 separates feed rotation and spin control; 1690 emphasizes a compact turret path and wide motion range; 1706 uses twin turret/spindexer streams. These designs should be compared on rate stability, jam recovery, aiming under disturbance, service time and total mass. Selecting a turret family is a commitment to its feed geometry, not an isolated decision about aiming.

## The later meta: supply, denial and survivability

2056's interview explicitly describes increasing emphasis on stealing fuel by Ontario provincials. The [5687 pre-Worlds strategic essay](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/an-outliers-take-on-the-26-worlds-strategic-situation/519681) discusses supply and defensive possibilities, but is a forecast and must not be cited as an observed Einstein result. The actual Einstein commentary reviewed here reports trench blocking, fuel supply roles, elevated shot obstruction and autonomous traffic failures.

That combination changes the mechanism requirements. A high scorer needs either a durable way to reach fuel or a reliable supplier. A defender may attack transit or pickup rather than merely push the shooter while it fires. An alliance needs timed delivery: a hopper arriving after the last usable shot interval cannot realize its theoretical value. Backup robot roles, remote shot geometry and the ability to recover from beaching become real architectural concerns.

The [official final-3 score table](https://frc-events.firstinspires.org/2026/CMPTX/playoffs/16) is particularly revealing. Daly records 140 and 171 fuel in its own two active shifts versus Newton's 12 and 67, while neither alliance records tower points. The shared-endgame fuel totals are much closer. This locates the large difference in time; it does not, by itself, prove whether route denial, breakdown, supply or aiming caused it. A visual audit remains necessary before assigning causality to any mechanism or defender.

## Converting the user's proposed rebuild into a design brief

“2910-style slapdown, Citrus-style expanding hopper, four-wide drum with 254 geometry” is a useful retrieval query, but the specific year and version are mandatory. The two published 2910 briefs resolve the version distinction: original BLITZ used mirrored four-bars; RE-BLITZ explicitly replaced the four-bar with a slapdown. Retrieve the rebuild geometry rather than blending the two machines. Likewise, the expansion linkage and scorer entry must be checked together.

The resulting high-level goals should state: target scored fuel per window; useful full-load capacity in each travel state; collection time from dispersed fuel; emptying time including the final few balls; range and defended-shot envelope; loaded bump/trench route access; current/energy budget; impact survival; repair time; and autonomous compatibility. Only then choose the detailed linkage, lane width and wheel geometry. Treat published BPS as reported observations until measurement conditions, fuel wear and accuracy are known.

For PCH, the 2026 event samples show a substantial rise from week 3 to week 5 and DCMP. That is a real development target, not an argument to install the final elite architecture immediately without test time. The most useful rebuild borrows the mature flow principles while preserving enough schedule to validate them under full-load, low-battery and defended conditions.


# REBUILT: high throughput must survive the inventory cycle

The base chapter provides the rules and stage benchmarks. This supplement connects the reviewed early-season RoboReplay predictions with later team explanations and reported match context. The earlier checkpoint reached the week-2 viewer analysis. The current checkpoint adds complete available-caption reads of the upper-bracket final, finals 1–3 and Einstein matches 3 and 6. Nine more late-season exports have been recovered but are not yet reviewed. This is still not a visual audit or an account of every late-season match. The detailed source accounts and uncertainties are in docs/roboreplay/-Cm-RuiSNlU.md and docs/roboreplay/Oo5nP00wVBM.md.

## A hopper stores time only under specific conditions

A starting inventory can let a robot keep firing while it searches for the next useful pile. That is the intuition behind calling capacity a time buffer. But the conversion depends on the firing rate, the collection rate, whether collection and firing can overlap, and how much of the nominal inventory is actually feedable. A hopper whose last fuel bridges above the entry does not deliver its photographed capacity. A hopper that must be emptied before the robot can use a route also stores a more constrained resource.

For a simplified interval with firing rate `s` and simultaneous intake rate `a`, the depletion time of a useful starting inventory `H` is `H / (s - a)` while `s > a` and those rates remain valid. With no simultaneous acquisition it is `H / s`. These are explanatory bounds, not predictions of a real robot: misses, jams, alignment pauses, acceleration and changing fuel density all change the rates. The useful design question is which assumption dominates the team’s intended play.

This model also explains why “more BPS” can make a supply problem worse. A faster scorer empties the same buffer sooner. If replenishment or access is the limiting process, a new drum may increase bench output while leaving active-window score almost unchanged. The team should measure full acquisition-to-discharge cycles and time spent starved before committing to the higher-rate architecture.

## Fixed shooters and turrets expose different dependencies

A fixed multi-lane shooter can put many fuel through at once, but needs a useful chassis orientation and a reliable way to preserve or recover that orientation under contact. A turret can allow more acquisition directions while aiming, but needs continuous singulation, usable angular range and calibration that survives motion. Neither description establishes the winner. The comparison depends on the achievable rate multiplied by the time that rate remains accurate and supplied.

The reviewed early videos discuss bracing a fixed shooter against field geometry, using blind areas, self-passing and mobile firing. These are observations and forecasts tied to particular configurations. Bracing can reduce rotational disturbance while constraining escape; a turret can compensate for heading changes while still being starved or prevented from reaching fuel. A defender can attack transit or inventory instead of directly defeating the aiming mechanism.

The 4414 deep note provides a complementary later team account: small turret swept volume, large low rotor and storage integration, with empirical calibration and a deliberately chosen autonomous. The 254, 1678, 2910 and 1768 dossiers expose different floor, preacceleration and rebuild choices. A recommendation should compare those complete packages rather than pretend a turret is an accessory to any hopper or a wide drum can replace any shooter without changing the floor.

## Storage and mobility are a state machine

Trench access is not just an overall height specification. The robot may have a compact travel state and an expanded scoring state; flexible walls, retained fuel, intake position and moving floor geometry affect whether the transition is safe. The loaded state can change the center of mass, ground interaction and the ability to slide off fuel. A mechanism that fits when empty can fail when the hopper is packed or when a wall deflects during contact.

The 1768 note shows how a lower four-bar link carried hopper geometry while satisfying several constraints. The 1678 dossier explains why its expansion and path had to be developed together. Those are examples of coupled design, not interchangeable parts. Before borrowing the collector from one team and storage from another, define the physical handoff between them in both directions and through every intermediate state.

Useful tests include partial fill, full fill, final-fuel evacuation, a forced reverse, a sideways hit and loaded travel over representative terrain. Also test a deployment that is interrupted halfway through. The question is not whether the mechanism eventually reaches both endpoints; it is whether the robot stays useful when the match prevents the ideal sequence.

## Scoring windows make return timing a mechanism requirement

An inactive period can be spent acquiring, passing, stealing, defending, repairing the field’s supply pattern or returning to the next scoring position. The last choice can be easy to undervalue because it adds no immediate points. A full hopper that arrives late can miss the interval that made the capacity valuable. A robot that empties quickly can tolerate a later arrival only if its approach and aim are reliable.

The reviewed kickoff and build-season videos forecast advantages from shift order and transition preparation. Later early-season reviews discuss actual delays, beaching and matched opportunities to assess the score. Preserve that chronology: a prediction that winning auto always determines the match is too strong, while an observed difference in preparation time can still be important. Current score before equal opportunities can mislead a coach; the remaining inventory and route access matter as well.

4414’s team explanation of rejecting double-swipe auto variants is a useful counterweight to a simple “collect more in auto” rule. The stated constraints include available firing time, transition endpoint and partner compatibility. That is a full-system optimization. It should be re-evaluated if the opponent does not create the expected fuel distribution or if a local partner occupies a different route.

## Resource control changes the value of a third robot

A supplier can add score indirectly by keeping a strong scorer’s useful area replenished. A defender can add value by denying replenishment, a route or the ability to return before the shift. A third scorer can instead reduce congestion by working a different region, or can create more traffic than output if all three need the same pile. The right role depends on measured supply and partner capability.

The early 2910 analysis is a dated passing hypothesis. The later team brief explicitly reports inadequate overall performance, partner dependence, unnecessary path travel and a complete rebuild. Preserve both. The original argument was not nonsensical; it depended on assumptions that did not satisfy the team’s actual schedule and operating targets. An agent should identify that dependency and offer a test, rather than present the final successful architecture as the only idea anyone could reasonably have chosen.

The later 7415 discussion adds a distinct, out-of-sample example of a swappable hopper and opponent preparation. It belongs as a strategic case with attributed team claims, not as an extra top-25 dossier or proof that every alliance should swap its envelope. Modular capability has value only if it remains legal, reliable, practiced and compatible with the rest of the robot when changed.

## Converting high-end examples into PCH goals

Set goals for usable capacity, collection from realistic fuel distributions, delivered fuel per active window, defended-shot accuracy, route access, jam recovery, current draw and repair time. Associate each number with the configuration and fuel condition that produced it. Fresh-fuel bench rate, worn-fuel event rate and full-match average are different records. The 1768 October feeder improvements and 254’s reported firing range should never be collapsed into one universal drum rate.

Use the historical week-3, week-5 and DCMP references as observed alliance outcomes. They do not tell an individual robot how much it must score alone. Choose the contribution your likely partners need and keep a path to the later stage. For a rebuild, preserve enough time to validate the whole chain. A smaller, tested change to an intake, floor or feed interface can be more valuable than adding another lane that the robot cannot supply. At Worlds and Einstein, return to the actual lineups and match-specific evidence; the final score by itself cannot identify why one architecture prevailed.


## Six late-season match accounts: architecture changes the next supply state

The recovered [May finals analysis](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Cm-RuiSNlU) and [August retrospective](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Oo5nP00wVBM) provide a more detailed account of the fuel-control problem. Both sources were read through their complete available captions. The match players did not render, so the actions below remain the commentator’s account. The distinction matters: a creator can accurately describe a movement while speculating about its intent or the cause of a failure. The long studies retain those separate layers and timestamp every explanation.

In the upper-bracket Daly–Newton meeting, the source connects 1323’s angled autonomous entry, 2910’s disrupted route, 4065’s midfield endpoint and the dual moving cleanup of 1323/4414. Its central argument is a chain: early possession changes neutral-zone availability; scarcity forces an opponent to collect in a less convenient location; that departure location makes its next steal later; the delay gives the prepared scorer more uncontested time. The chain is useful as an audit hypothesis. It is not established by one score difference and does not mean every link was deliberately planned. The official table also records Newton winning its second own active shift and final-period fuel; do not rewrite Daly’s overall win as winning every window.

The first final offers a different state. The source reports 1323’s interruption and praises 4414 for collecting through the transition instead of matching immediate scoring. Daly’s recorded transition fuel is zero against Newton’s 57. The purpose in the creator’s account is to change the next supply state, not to score in that interval. Whether that choice would win with six healthy robots remains a counterfactual. The relevant architecture question is whether a robot can preserve enough inventory and route flexibility to turn deferred scoring into useful output before its next window ends.

The second final adds a defensive geometry account. The source describes 4065 keeping 2910 near a bump, hub and trench bar where the deployed collector limits rotations, then using a blocker even after a crossing succeeds. Newton also changes tactics: a successful early fuel contest, a delayed 4414 escape, holding fuel out of the shared pool and passing instead of taking another heavily defended cycle. The creator considers some of those adaptations valuable even though Newton loses. The audit must measure opportunity cost and actual outcomes before turning them into a universal policy. A pin described as worth its penalties is a commentary judgment, not permission to ignore the governing rules.

The third final changes autonomous matchups and includes reported 2046 shooting trouble and 868 damage. Those failure states prevent treating the 306-point result as a pure turret-versus-drum experiment. Official own-active-shift fuel totals are 311–79, while final-period fuel is 154–161 and tower points are zero for both sides. The source’s account directs attention to access, supply, blocked approaches and operational symptoms. It cannot diagnose current draw, voltage sag or the exact fuel denied by a defender.

The August examples add a configuration-specific mobility distinction. A robot may be trench-capable when compact but bump-only with an extended hopper. A steal can be attractive on the way in yet poor if the exit crosses a defended bottleneck after the hopper expands. The source describes 254 briefly giving up its own scoring to let 8613 reach 2481’s return, and 6324 sacrificing its next collection to delay a loaded 1678. Those are partner timing trades, not evidence that scorers should generically stop scoring to defend. The same account highlights 4946’s curved bumper perimeter as an escape hypothesis and 6324’s picks as changing the defender’s problem. Exact geometry, friction and intent remain unverified.

Finally, the retrospective argues that losing autonomous narrowly may improve later opportunities. That does not erase the earlier example of Daly compounding an auto win. The conditions differ: who retains the neutral zone, who can stage fuel, how loaded robots cross and whether the next scorer has to search. The agent should model both possible autonomous outcomes and the resulting inventories and routes. It should not optimize toward deliberately losing auto from one close match, or optimize toward winning auto while ignoring the terminal positions that determine the next minute.
