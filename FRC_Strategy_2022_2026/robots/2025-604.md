## The Flush in-season release · drivetrain

### Published construction or code

29-inch square chassis with WCP X2i modules and a 5.9:1 drive ratio; the team optimized for roughly 18-foot average sprint distance. Protective panels isolate electronics from falling coral.

### Stated rationale and boundaries

Stability at high reach and the expected travel distance drive the stated choices.

### Engineering interpretation

Optimize acceleration and usable travel under the expected route distribution, rather than unloaded top speed alone. A larger support polygon can help a reaching superstructure while increasing congestion exposure. The published dimensions and ratio are a configuration snapshot; they do not prove the same ratio is best for 1833 or a different game.

## The Flush elevator and arm · scoring

### Published construction or code

Single-stage elevator uses 2x2 uprights, cross bracing and chain drive on both sides. The release gives two Kraken X60s, 3:1 and 28 inches of motion; stator limits increased from 40A to 80A. The arm is rigidly tied side-to-side and its gearbox shimmed; the team describes detuning and reducing the drive to one motor at 100A to avoid tipping.

### Stated rationale and boundaries

Rigidity, low backlash and precise automated control are explicit priorities.

### Engineering interpretation

Motor count and theoretical joint acceleration cannot be separated from robot stability. Dual-sided chain drive addresses carriage twist, while a rigid camera mount helps relate vision measurements to mechanism geometry. Preserve the stated revisions and the release’s mixed original/final motor annotations; do not silently combine every number into one certified event configuration.

## The Flush station funnel and gripper · intake

### Published construction or code

A full-width 4mm corrugated-plastic funnel loads from three sides through separately powered rollers. The gripper has independently powered left/right rollers, four coral rollers and two algae rollers, plus an open top.

### Stated rationale and boundaries

The team wanted tolerant loading, horizontal L1 packing and precise placement. The open top avoids reef entanglement and permits a shallower coral release angle.

### Engineering interpretation

A funnel can absorb human-player variability while the gripper controls the final release. Independent sides support a different L1 placement policy rather than merely increasing raw roller power. This is an architectural option for a station-fed role, not evidence that floor coral pickup was unnecessary for every alliance. Corrugated plastic stiffness, folding and clearances must be evaluated in the installed package.

## The Flush climb/funnel interface · endgame

### Published construction or code

The release describes a roughly 3.5-pound climber including motor and gearbox, stowed below the funnel, surgical-tubing extension and a 15:1 winch with a 2:1 pulley. A rear funnel ramp unfolds to clear the cage and climb.

### Stated rationale and boundaries

Compact packaging preserves the loading system while providing cage engagement tolerance.

### Engineering interpretation

The climb is coupled to the funnel’s motion and the loaded robot CG. A compact mechanism is not necessarily easy to approach under traffic; validate capture, retraction and failures with the actual funnel and piece state. These are team-reported design quantities, not measured masses or an exhaustive installed-version history.

## The Flush and separate Quixshot offseason machine · controls

### Published construction or code

The Flush uses predictive target selection, smooth handover from driver motion, reef-clearance sequencing and separate local/global localization. The team reports 547/549 L2-L4 coral placements over its last two events. Quixshot is a distinct offseason coral launcher with manually tuned launch velocity differentials and a one-time intake deploy latch.

### Stated rationale and boundaries

The team reports that packaging a ground pickup with the in-season climber became difficult, so the compatible pickup was instead installed on the offseason launcher.

### Engineering interpretation

Do not attach Quixshot’s launcher to the in-season elevator robot. The 99.6% is a bounded team-reported placement statistic, not full-match reliability or 99.6% for every task. The published offseason answer says it did not deep-climb or handle algae at that point. The strongest transferable lesson is a deliberate interface and packaging trade, not the assertion that launching always beats placing.

Verify the installed configuration and interfaces against the original publication/CAD; prototype full acquisition-to-output cycles, recovery and service access under the intended loaded and defended conditions. These tests are proposed, not performed.

## Distinct offseason machine

Quixshot is a separate robot. The team’s July reply explains that a ground intake intended for the in-season machine conflicted with climber packaging and was installed on the launcher instead. Launch velocity differentials were manually tuned. The first offseason answer explicitly says no algae or deep climb at that point.
